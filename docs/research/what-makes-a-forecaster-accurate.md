# What makes a forecaster accurate

Research note for ticket 10. Sources opened 7-8 October 2026. It covers what is published about building accurate forecasters with language models, and what the trial-forecasting and meta-research literature says about which inputs carry signal, so that the Span forecaster starts from evidence.

## Summary

1. Retrieval is the largest single lever in general event forecasting (about 0.02 Brier in the one clean ablation, far more on live markets), but for trial outcomes retrieval of similar trials gave mixed results on CT Open, because the answer is in no document.
2. What is done with retrieved comparable trials matters more than retrieving them: stepwise adjustment from seven distinct historical trials beat raw retrieval by 7 to 8 macro-F1 points on CT Open's benchmark, and by 1 to 4 points from a single one (one round, run by the benchmark's own authors).
3. Averaging 5 to 10 independent runs is worth doing and the averaging rule does not matter; a language model asked to pick or merge the runs does worse than the plain mean unless it gathers new evidence.
4. Miscalibration has no fixed direction: retrieval systems built on 2023-2025 models hedged toward 0.5, while frontier models asked closed-book in late 2025 were overconfident and all but one lost to a constant base rate.
5. Post-hoc correction helped only as a fixed one-parameter adjustment; fitted or flexible corrections did worse out of sample, and a few hundred trials cannot support more than one or two fitted parameters.
6. For humans the case for starting from a base rate is good but comes from bundled training; for language models a prompt instruction to use base rates is worth at most 0.01-0.02 Brier and is not robust, while supplying the number from outside the model is what the stronger systems share.
7. Structured reasoning prompts mostly do nothing; explicit Bayesian updating and propose-then-select made forecasts measurably worse, and more reasoning effort did not improve calibration.
8. Every positive fine-tuning result used at least about 6,000 training examples; nothing supports fine-tuning a language model on a few hundred trials, where a small tabular model or in-context use is the supported option.
9. Numeric intervals stated by language models are far too narrow (95% intervals covering 9-44%, 99% intervals covering 65%); recalibrating widths on held-out data repairs coverage; no study scores hazard-ratio forecasts.
10. In the trial domain a random forest matched language models, registry features explain almost none of the variance (Nagelkerke R² 0.038), a positive phase 2 is the most consistent predictor, and oncologists lost to a base-rate lookup in one study and beat an uninformative forecast in another.

## How to read the source tags

Each claim is followed by its source and one tag:

- **[full]** the full text was opened and the passages behind the claim were read directly. It does not mean every page was read.
- **[abstract]** abstract only. Biomedical abstracts were retrieved from PubMed and are linked by DOI.
- **[snippet]** search-result snippet only; the source itself was not opened.
- **[first-party]** a post or code published by the benchmark's owner, opened and read; not peer reviewed.

Scores are Brier scores unless stated; lower is better. "Closed-book" means no retrieval or tools.

## 1. Retrieval

**Evidence that it helps, and by how much**

- In the only end-to-end ablation on a clean post-cutoff test set (914 binary questions opened after 1 June 2023), the full system scored 0.179 against a human crowd at 0.149. Removing fine-tuning gave 0.186; removing retrieval as well gave 0.206, which is the level of the best closed-book model (0.208). Retrieval was therefore worth about 0.020, fine-tuning about 0.007, and ensembling about 0.003 on validation. [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) **[full]**
- The same retrieval pipeline, applied by ForecastBench to a different question mix, did not help: "Access to recent topical news related to the questions did not improve performance." Claude 3.5 Sonnet scored 0.122 with the crowd forecast supplied and a scratchpad prompt, and 0.127 with news added. Supplying the crowd forecast mattered more: the best model without it scored 0.136. [Karger et al., ForecastBench, v5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19839) **[full]**
- A 2025 industry system reported, on ForecastBench questions, 0.1230 with no search, 0.1217 and 0.1174 with one-shot search over two providers, and 0.1182 and 0.1140 with agentic search over the same two. On 64 live prediction markets that closed during the test, with market prices withheld, it scored 0.1002 with search and 0.3609 without, against 0.1111 for the market price. Retrieval matters most where the question turns on recent information, agentic search beat one-shot search for both providers, and the provider mattered about as much as the search style. [Alur et al., AIA Forecaster technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07678) **[full]**; a company report, not peer reviewed.
- Retrieval from an offline news corpus improved accuracy by 9-18% across model families on open-ended questions, even with articles cut off one month before resolution. [Chandak et al., OpenForecaster](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.25070) **[full]**
- Model quality dominates scaffolding in live tournaments. Metaculus's template bots (one search, a roughly 30-line prompt, five runs aggregated) placed second overall in Q2 2025, and few entrants beat the template bot running the same model. [Metaculus Q2 2025 results](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/F2stjK9wHSy3HPEC9/q2-ai-benchmark-results-pros-maintain-clear-lead) **[first-party]**. On ForecastBench in January 2026 a minimal entry (model plus standard search tools, eight forecasts averaged) tied for second with a multi-stage retrieval pipeline. [Forecasting Research Institute, January 2026](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/llms-are-closing-the-gap-on-human) **[first-party]**; the methods are the teams' own descriptions, not ablations.
- Retrieval does not remove the decay of a model's knowledge over time. [Dai et al., Daily Oracle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.08324) **[abstract]**

**Designs that were tested**

- Query generation: Halawi et al. prompt the model two ways (direct expansion, and decomposition into sub-questions) and take the union of queries. Ranking: a cheaper model rates each article's relevance from its title and first 250 words, which the authors report saved 70% of cost at high recall. Summarisation: each kept article is condensed with respect to the question. Number of documents: a sweep over 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 summaries, ordered by relevance or recency, found 15 by relevance best (0.177 on validation) and 20 similar. The system closed on the crowd when at least five relevant articles were found. [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) **[full]**
- OpenForecaster found gains plateau after five retrieved chunks of 512 tokens. [Chandak et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.25070) **[full]**
- Web search can hurt numeric estimates: in a small test (25 questions per dataset, four models) search degraded estimates overall (win rate 39.8%, p = 0.009) and helped only the weakest model. [Hobor et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01896) **[full]**; the authors call it preliminary.

**Trial outcomes specifically**

- CT Open tested retrieval of similar completed trials from ClinicalTrials.gov. On endpoint questions (macro-F1) it moved o3-mini from 68.40 to 76.88 in the Winter 2025 round and from 59.83 to 59.07 in Summer 2025; it moved Claude Opus 4.5 from 53.71 to 58.69 in Summer 2025. The authors call the gains "limited and inconsistent". An agent with web search, run only with o3-mini because it cost more than $1,000, scored 64.83 in Winter (below the 68.40 closed-book score) and 61.75 in Summer (above 59.83). [Wang et al., CT Open, v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16742) **[full]**
- The stated reason retrieval failed: "the retrieved similar trials were not themselves irrelevant; rather, the model appeared unable to properly account for subtle but consequential differences between the retrieved trials and the target trial." A worked case had a model retrieve the right comparator trial and miss that the target enrolled the subgroup the comparator had excluded. Same source **[full]**
- A follow-up from the same group fed the same retrieved trials through a stepwise procedure: start from one historical trial with a known result and change one factor at a time (population, arms, outcome measure, eligibility) until it matches the target. On 701 superiority questions from CT Open Summer 2025, GPT-5 scored 72.4 with raw retrieval, 69.4 with no retrieval, 76.2 with one anchor trial, 80.8 with seven different anchors, and 75.8 with seven runs from the same anchor. [Zheng et al., DeepImagine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23054) **[full]**; one benchmark round, authors overlap with CT Open, metric is macro-F1 on per-outcome superiority questions, not a probability score on the primary endpoint.

**Strength.** Good that retrieval helps general forecasting when recent information decides the question, with effect size depending heavily on the question mix. Weak and mixed for trial outcomes. No retrieval ablation exists for phase 3 primary endpoints scored by Brier.

## 2. Aggregation

**Several runs**

- Six forecasts per question, validation set: mean 0.1656, median 0.1651, geometric mean 0.1655, trimmed mean 0.1649, a language model asked to merge the six 0.1691, single forecasts 0.1676. The merging model was worse than no ensemble. [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) **[full]**
- Ten forecasts per question: mean 0.1140, trimmed mean 0.1142, median 0.1138, one forecast 0.1182. Resampling from 50 forecasts per question showed a sharp fall in Brier from one to five runs and a modest further fall to fifteen; the authors standardise on ten. They add that scoring a single run per question "yields highly variable performance" even averaged over 1,610 questions, so uncertainty computed over questions alone understates the true variability. [Alur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07678) **[full]**; the run-count result is described in text from a figure, with no table.
- Synthesis by a language model, same report: a model constrained to pick the best of ten scored 0.1191 and a model free to merge them 0.1168, both worse than the mean (0.1140); only a supervisor that ran new searches to resolve disagreements did better (0.1125). Same source **[full]**
- A 2026 agent paper uses five runs, finds gains rising with the number of runs, and finds that a cross-validated rule for shrinking toward 0.5 when runs disagree selects no shrinkage. [Murphy, BLF, v5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18576) **[full]**; single-author preprint.
- Practice elsewhere: five runs (Metaculus template bots), eight (the xAI ForecastBench entry), seven ([Turtel et al. 2025b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17989) **[full]**).

**Several models, and diversity**

- The median of twelve models scored 0.20 against a human crowd at 0.19 on 31 questions, not significantly different; the study is small. [Schoenegger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379) **[full]**
- Letting models read each other's forecasts and revise reduced log loss by 0.020 (about 4%, p = 0.017) when the models differed, and did nothing when three copies of one model deliberated. [Schneider and Schramm 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22625) **[abstract]**
- In the trial domain, seven runs from different anchor trials beat seven runs from the same anchor by 3-5 macro-F1 points, and same-anchor repetition added almost nothing over a single run. [Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23054) **[full]**. Gain comes from different evidence, not from more samples of the same reasoning.

**Model plus a human crowd or other reference**

- Averaging the system with the crowd at 1:4 improved the crowd from 0.149 to 0.146; an equal-weight average beat the crowd in every subset examined. [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) **[full]**
- Showing GPT-4 and Claude 2 the human median improved them by 17-28%, "though this leads to less accurate predictions than simply averaging human and machine forecasts". [Schoenegger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379) **[full]**. Arithmetic blending outside the model beat asking the model to update.
- An ensemble of the AIA system with the market price beat the market alone on a benchmark where the system alone lost to the market. [Alur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07678) **[full]** for the statement; the regression numbers were not extracted.

**Extremising**

- Averages of human forecasts improve when pushed away from 0.5, for two reasons: the probability scale compresses at its ends, and each forecaster, holding partial information, sensibly understates. For self-described experts the best exponent on the mean was about 2.4 and improved Brier from about 0.187 to 0.148. The authors also report later cases where the best exponent was below 1, "presumably because the judgments are initially much too extreme". [Baron et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/deca.2014.0293) **[full]**; table values were reconstructed from text that had lost its decimal points.
- A one-parameter logit aggregator beat common alternatives on 69 events forecast by over 1,300 people. [Satopää et al. 2014](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v30y2014i2p344-356.html) **[abstract]**
- The second reason for extremising assumes forecasters hold different information. Repeated runs of one model on one evidence set do not, so the human result does not transfer automatically. This is inference from the mechanism, not a tested result.

**Strength.** Good for averaging about five to ten runs and for the irrelevance of the averaging rule. Good that a strong outside reference blended in arithmetically adds value. No evidence on aggregating numeric distributions.

## 3. Calibration

**Are the probabilities calibrated?**

- Closed-book models in early 2024 were "not well calibrated in the zero-shot setting"; the retrieval system was close to calibrated, with the residual error being underconfidence. It rarely output low probabilities, and scored 0.04 worse than the crowd on questions where the crowd was within 0.05 of 0 or 1. [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) **[full]**
- The AIA report describes the same hedging toward 0.5 as systematic and says prompt changes were "largely ineffective at mitigating this problem". [Alur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07678) **[full]**
- The opposite was found for frontier models asked closed-book in late 2025 on 300 Kalshi questions with a 40% base rate: all five were overconfident (expected calibration error 0.120 to 0.395), only one beat the base rate (Brier skill score 0.057), and the extended-reasoning configuration was worst (skill score -0.799) despite a prompt that told models to be calibrated. [Nel, KalshiBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16030) **[full]**; single-author preprint, 300 questions.
- Earlier closed-book models also over-predicted "yes": mean forecasts were significantly above 50% on a set where 45% resolved yes. [Schoenegger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379) **[full]**. Stated confidence is overconfident across general tasks. [Xiong et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063) **[abstract]**
- One 2026 team reports using no post-hoc calibration because current models are "broadly well-calibrated". [Forecasting Research Institute](https://forecastingresearch.substack.com/p/llms-are-closing-the-gap-on-human) **[first-party]**, relaying the team's own account.

The direction and size of miscalibration depend on the model generation, on whether evidence is supplied, and on the question mix. They have to be measured on the forecaster actually built.

**Post-hoc fixes that were tested**

- Binning and isotonic regression did not improve an already near-calibrated system. [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) **[full]**
- On a hedging system: no correction 0.1140; Platt scaling with a coefficient fixed in advance at √3, 0.1076; Platt scaling fitted on a different benchmark, 0.1104; isotonic regression fitted on a different benchmark, 0.1134; linear regression 0.1125. The fixed one-parameter correction beat every fitted one out of sample, and the authors declined to use their tuned coefficient (2.27) to avoid overfitting. The largest gains came from forecasts in the 0.2-0.4 and 0.6-0.8 ranges. They note the correction pushes a forecast on the wrong side of 0.5 further the wrong way. [Alur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07678) **[full]**
- Platt scaling gave small gains on a weaker model and no resolved gain on the strongest, and gains were smaller when a crowd forecast was supplied, "since that tends to act as a very informative prior, reducing the need for calibration". [Murphy, BLF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18576) **[full]**
- Training on resolved questions improves calibration directly (expected calibration error 0.089 to 0.062). [Turtel et al. 2025b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17989) **[full]**

**Shrinking toward a base rate.** No paper was found that tests shrinking a language model's forecast toward a reference-class base rate. The nearest evidence is indirect: supplying a crowd forecast or an empirical prior helps weaker systems and reduces the need for calibration (ForecastBench, BLF above); most closed-book frontier models lose to a constant base rate (KalshiBench); and in oncology a base-rate algorithm scored 0.19 where individual oncologists scored 0.29 ([Benjamin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1634/theoncologist.2020-0054) **[abstract]**).

**Small samples**

- With fewer than about 200-1,000 calibration cases, Platt scaling outperformed isotonic regression for all nine learning methods tested, because isotonic regression is less constrained and overfits. [Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana 2005](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~alexn/papers/calibration.icml05.crc.rev3.pdf) **[full]**
- "A flexible assessment of calibration in small validation data sets is problematic." [Van Calster et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2015.12.005) **[abstract]**
- Every extremising result above is centred on 0.5 and was estimated on general-event questions. A trial forecaster's natural centre is its base rate (about 64% for industry-led trials in the project's hand-traced sample), so those coefficients should not be carried over. This is inference, not a tested result.

**Strength.** Good that miscalibration is real and that its direction varies. Moderate that a single fixed parameter is the most that survives out of sample. Absent for base-rate shrinkage and for anything fitted on a few hundred trials.

## 4. Base rates and reference classes

**Humans**

- A training module of under an hour, which taught comparison classes and base rates among ten principles, improved Brier scores by 6 to 11% over controls in each of four tournament years. Among trained forecasters, explanations that invoked comparison classes went with more accurate forecasts, and in an analysis of variance comparison classes were the only principle significantly associated with better scores (post-mortems and "other perspectives" went with worse ones). [Chang et al. 2016](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S1930297500004599/type/journal_article) **[full]**. The training was a bundle and the per-principle result is self-reported and correlational.
- Top forecasters' written rationales use more comparison classes, more past-focused language and more weighing of opposed considerations. [Karvetski et al., manuscript of the 2022 International Journal of Forecasting paper](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/SSRN-id3779404.pdf) **[full]** for the abstract and introduction; effect sizes not extracted.
- Training, teaming and tracking each improved accuracy in the first two tournament years, and the training encouraged reference classes. Mellers et al. 2014, Psychological Science 25(5):1106-1115 **[snippet]**; not opened.
- For trial outcomes: 137 oncologists forecasting primary-outcome attainment scored 0.29 on average, worse than always saying 50% (0.25) and worse than an algorithm using base rates (0.19); their discrimination was nil (area under the curve 0.52). [Benjamin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1634/theoncologist.2020-0054) **[abstract]**. In a second study 148 experts forecasting 25 trials scored 0.21-0.22, better than 50%, with areas under the curve of 0.70-0.73, and feedback training made no difference. [Doussau et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1177/17407745231203375) **[abstract]**. The two disagree on whether experts discriminate; both are small.

**Language models**

- Across 38 prompts, four models and 100 ForecastBench questions, closed-book, a "base rate first" prompt changed Brier by -0.011 in the preregistered mixed model (not significant after correction) and by -0.016 in the simpler preregistered t-test (adjusted p = 0.036). A frequency-format prompt was similar (-0.014 and -0.019). In a second study that combined the best prompts and added o1 and o1-mini, no prompt, including base rate first plus frequency reasoning, improved accuracy after correction. [Schoenegger et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01578) **[full]**
- An independent test of strategies drawn from the superforecasting literature found none beat a basic prompt (0.1221): base rates 0.1562, both sides 0.1553, decomposition 0.1895, simulated crowd 0.1706. With questions reweighted so that yes and no outcomes counted equally, the base-rate strategy was the worst of all (0.2540 against 0.1955 for the basic prompt and 0.1746 for the human market). The authors attribute the basic prompt's raw score to a bias toward low probabilities on a mostly-no question set. [Pratt et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04446) **[full]**; which model was used was not confirmed.
- Halawi et al.'s best scratchpad prompt ends by asking the model to check for over- or underconfidence and to consider base rates, but the step was never ablated alone. The KalshiBench prompt asked models to consider base rates, and four of five still lost to the base rate.
- What stronger systems share is a number supplied from outside the model, not an instruction: the crowd forecast in ForecastBench (0.136 without, 0.122 with), and an empirical base rate per question type in BLF, where a baseline using only crowd forecasts and empirical priors, with no language model, was beaten significantly by only one of four agent systems compared. [Murphy, BLF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18576) **[full]**
- The winning Metaculus bot of Q2 2025 wrote separate "outside view" and "inside view" research reports before forecasting. It did not beat the plain template bot on the same model within a 95% interval. [Metaculus Q2 2025 results](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/F2stjK9wHSy3HPEC9/q2-ai-benchmark-results-pros-maintain-clear-lead) **[first-party]**
- Anchoring on specific historical trials and adjusting stepwise is the trial-domain version of reference-class reasoning with a positive result (section 1, DeepImagine).

**Strength.** Moderate for humans, with the base-rate component never isolated experimentally. Weak for language models when delivered as a prompt instruction. No study supplies a reference-class base rate to a model and asks it to adjust for a specific trial.

## 5. Reasoning scaffolds

- Most do nothing. In the 38-prompt study's preregistered mixed model, no prompt improved on a minimal control after correction: chain of thought (-0.011), arguments for and against (-0.009), step-back (-0.011), pre-mortem, self-critique, simulated debate and simulated dialogue were all indistinguishable from it. In the simpler preregistered t-test three prompts survived correction (frequency format, base rate first and step-back, each between -0.015 and -0.019). Two made forecasts worse in both analyses: a prompt to reason as a Bayesian from a prior (+0.030) and a prompt to propose several answers, evaluate them and select one (+0.033). In the second study, which added o1 and o1-mini, the Halawi-style scratchpad was indistinguishable from control (+0.003), a superforecaster-written conditional odds prompt was worse (+0.023), and no prompt was better. [Schoenegger et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01578) **[full]**; closed-book, 100 questions, temperature 0.
- For GPT-4 closed-book, zero-shot scored 0.208 and scratchpad 0.209. [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) **[full]**. ForecastBench, by contrast, notes that most of its top configurations used the scratchpad prompt. [Karger et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19839) **[full]**
- Decomposition was the worst strategy in Pratt et al. (0.1895 against 0.1221 for the basic prompt). [Pratt et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04446) **[full]**
- Explicit Bayesian updating using likelihoods elicited from the model scored well below the same agent updating its belief in plain language (Brier Index 63-66 against 70.4, higher is better). A structured running summary of evidence for and against helped two Gemini models and not a GPT model, and reduced the spread between runs (within-question standard deviation 0.094 to 0.030 for one model). [Murphy, BLF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18576) **[full]**
- More reasoning effort did not help calibration (KalshiBench, section 3), numeric estimates ([Hobor et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01896) **[full]**) or elicited priors ([Marzoev et al., OpenEstimate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15096) **[full]**).
- Debate and deliberation help only when participants differ: section 2 (Schneider and Schramm). A multi-agent design reports 12-18% Brier gains when each agent is given partly private evidence, and that removing the asymmetry removes most of the gain. [Li et al., InfoDelphi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01661) **[abstract]**. Outside forecasting, picking the best single response beat three deliberation protocols. [Kaushal and Singh, DeliberationBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.08835) **[abstract]**
- The one scaffold with a positive result in the trial domain is the stepwise comparison against anchor trials (section 1).

**Strength.** Good, from two independent prompt studies, a tournament and several system reports, that generic reasoning prompts are not where accuracy comes from, and that forcing explicit probability arithmetic can hurt. The trial-specific scaffold rests on one paper.

## 6. Fine-tuning versus prompting

| Study | Training data | Result | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Halawi et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18563) | 6,000 reasoning traces that beat the crowd, selected from 73,632 generated on 3,762 questions | 0.186 to 0.179 | full |
| [Turtel et al. 2025a](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05253) | 9,800 resolved Polymarket questions, self-play ranked by outcome | 0.221 to 0.200 and 0.212 to 0.197 for two 14B models, level with GPT-4o (0.196) | full |
| [Turtel et al. 2025b](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17989) | about 100,000 questions including synthetic ones, reinforcement learning | 0.215 to 0.190 with seven runs; better than o1 by 0.011; worse than the market by 0.039 | full |
| [Chandak et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.25070) | about 52,000 questions generated from news | an 8B model reaches much larger models; gains still rising with data size | full |
| [Reinisch et al., CTP-LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.10995) | about 6,000 trial protocols, GPT-3.5 | 67% accuracy on phase transition across phases, 75% for phase 3 to approval, on a balanced set | full |
| [Zheng et al., DeepImagine](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23054) | 127,000 trial-pair examples | a 12B model matches GPT-5 inside the same procedure | full |
| [Martinelli et al., Query2Effect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29631) | 72,000 effect-size questions | absolute error 27-71% lower than prompted models | abstract |

- No study was found that fine-tunes a language model for forecasting on fewer than about 6,000 examples, and none reports the result of trying with a few hundred. The absence is not evidence that it fails, but nothing supports it.
- What has been shown to work at a few hundred labels is a classical model. AutoCT uses a language model only to propose and extract features, then fits logistic regression, random forest or gradient boosting on 100 training and 100 validation trials per phase; on phase 3 it reached an area under the curve of 0.702 against 0.711-0.741 for published models trained on full benchmark training sets. [Liu et al., AutoCT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04293) **[full]**; tested on 100 trials, with comparison figures taken from other papers' test sets; labels are TrialBench approval-type outcomes; cost about $150 per feature search.
- CT Open's random forest was trained on 7,292 trials and matched language models (section 8).
- A temporal trap applies to any training on resolved questions: early examples leak information about later ones. [Paleka et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00723) **[full]**

**Strength.** Good that training on resolved questions helps at the scale of thousands to hundreds of thousands. Absent at a few hundred. With a few hundred labelled trials the supported uses are a small tabular model, a one-parameter recalibration, and worked examples in the prompt, the last of which is untested for trials.

## 7. Continuous quantities

**Language models**

- Asked for 99% intervals on Fermi-style questions, models covered the truth 65% of the time on average. Adjusting interval widths by split conformal prediction on 500 held-out questions restored 99% coverage and cut the interval score by 54%. [Epstein et al., FermiEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26995) **[full]**; small, cost-efficient models.
- Eleven models asked for population statistics with 95% intervals covered the truth 9-44% of the time. Conformal recalibration brought coverage near 95% where at least 15 calibration points per group were available. Larger models were more accurate; more reasoning effort was not. [Hobor et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01896) **[full]**
- A prior elicited from a frontier model was worth about five real samples from the target distribution and was overconfident. Combined with data it still helped: a model prior plus N samples beat an uninformative prior plus N samples in most cases at N = 5 (about 72-77% of cases across three domains), falling toward half at N = 30. The elicitation format that produced more uncertainty calibrated better; sampling strategy, reasoning effort and prompt design made little difference. [Marzoev et al., OpenEstimate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15096) **[full]**
- Interval length did not track the confidence level the model was asked for. [Bahaj et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.12098) **[abstract]**
- In a live tournament, bots trailed professional forecasters more on numeric questions than on binary ones (head-to-head score -23.2 against -14.8), on 21 numeric questions with no interval reported. [Metaculus Q2 2025 results](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/F2stjK9wHSy3HPEC9/q2-ai-benchmark-results-pros-maintain-clear-lead) **[first-party]**. The Metaculus template elicits six percentiles (10, 20, 40, 60, 80, 90) and tells the model that good forecasters "set wide 90/10 confidence intervals to account for unknown unknowns". [Metaculus bot template](https://github.com/Metaculus/metac-bot-template/blob/main/main.py) **[first-party]** code. No ablation of this format was found.

**Humans and trial effect sizes**

- Neurology experts forecasting trial effect sizes gave 95% intervals that contained the result for 44% of experts. [Atanasov et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1212/WNL.0000000000009819) **[abstract]**
- Sponsors' design assumptions are optimistic: across 253 cancer trials the assumed benefit exceeded the observed one by a median 13% in proportional terms. [Gan et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1093/jnci/djs141) **[abstract]**
- In 385 published phase 3 oncology trials the median ratio of observed to design hazard ratio was 1.09 (interquartile range 0.89-1.29): 1.08 (0.85-1.27) for industry-sponsored trials and 1.24 for cooperative-group trials. The median observed hazard ratio was 0.77 for industry-sponsored and 0.90 for other trials. [Noticewala et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/jnci/djaf097) **[full]**; published trials only, 2002-2020.
- Randomised phase 2 trials overstate: across 57 matched pairs, phase 2 hazard ratios were 26-27% more favourable than those of the phase 3 trial of the same regimen. [Liang et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejca.2019.08.006) **[abstract]**
- An estimate that is just significant overstates the true effect by a factor of about 1.7 on average, which is an argument for shrinking any earlier-phase estimate before using it. [van Zwet et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.9173) **[abstract]**

**A yardstick derived here, not a published number.** If the industry interquartile range of 0.85-1.27 is treated as normal on the log scale, the standard deviation of log(observed/design) is about 0.30, and an 80% interval spans the central value multiplied and divided by about 1.46. A forecaster holding the design hazard ratio and nothing else should therefore not give an 80% interval much tighter than that.

**Strength.** Good and consistent that language-model intervals are too narrow and that recalibrating widths on held-out data works. Nothing on hazard ratios: no paper scores a language model's forecast of one, and no study compares quantile elicitation formats for forecasting.

## 8. Trial forecasting: what the domain evidence says

**Language models against simpler methods**

- CT Open's summary: "simple ML and NN baselines remain competitive with, and sometimes outperform, LLM-based approaches". On endpoint questions the random forest scored 66.60 and 65.57 macro-F1 in the two rounds; the three proprietary models asked closed-book ranged from 65.29 to 70.17 in Winter 2025 and from 51.54 to 59.83 in Summer 2025, and two 14B open-weight models scored 52-54 in both. Adding numeric and categorical trial fields to the language-model prompt reduced performance, so the authors left them out. [Wang et al., CT Open](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16742) **[full]**
- Feature groups the tabular models relied on most, by permutation: the text of the endpoint, the comparator arm, the condition and the eligibility criteria; then trial locations and lead sponsor class. Same source **[full]**
- Contamination is visible: Claude Opus 4.5, whose knowledge cutoff post-dated the Winter 2025 round, outperformed o3-mini and GPT-5 on five of six measures in that round and underperformed them on four of six in the round it could not have seen. Same source **[full]**
- The CT Open paper has no numeric question and no human comparison, and reports macro-F1 and accuracy, not a probability score; its questions are per outcome measure, not per primary endpoint. Same source **[full]**
- Older benchmarks: HINT reports a phase 3 PR-AUC of 0.623 ([Fu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.04252) **[abstract]**). CTO builds labels from weak signals including news sentiment and sponsors' share prices and reports distribution shift in recent trials ([Gao et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10292) **[full]** for the labelling passages). A multi-agent GPT-4 system reports PR-AUC 0.79, a gain of 0.33 over a standard prompt ([Yue et al., ClinicalAgent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14777) **[abstract]**). None of these labels "met its primary endpoint", and all predate current model cutoffs.
- Language models recall published oncology results: given only a DOI, three model families classified 250 trials as positive or negative with accuracy 0.63-0.67 against 0.50 by chance, and 0.79-0.88 from the title. [Windisch et al. 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.02.26345347v1.full) **[full]** page, read through the fetch tool's summary and not passage by passage.

**Features that predict a positive phase 3 trial**

- Among 824 registered phase 3 oncology trials with public primary results, 43.4% met a primary endpoint. In the multivariable model: biomarker-based patient selection, odds ratio 1.75 (95% CI 1.22-2.51); started in or after 2012, 1.36; multiple primary endpoints, 1.68. Trial size and solid versus haematological tumour were not associated. Univariable: chemotherapy 0.48, targeted therapy 1.43, overall survival as primary endpoint 0.52, progression-free survival 1.89, event-free or disease-free survival 0.47, response 2.23, advanced versus adjuvant setting 1.57. By tumour: 14-18% for central nervous system, pancreatic and head and neck, up to 58% for melanoma and renal cell. The whole model's Nagelkerke R² was 0.038. [Yamamoto et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/cts.70635) **[full]**; sponsor type and earlier-phase results were not examined.
- Sponsor: published industry-sponsored trials met the primary endpoint 57% of the time against 30% for others. [Noticewala et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/jnci/djaf097) **[full]**
- Earlier-phase results: in 189 phase 3 trials with cited phase 2 support, a positive phase 2 correlated with a positive phase 3 (p = 0.03); randomised phase 2 trials predicted no better than single-arm ones, and phase 2 endpoint, sponsor and sample size did not predict. [Monzon et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejca.2015.08.004) **[abstract]**. In 57 matched pairs a positive randomised phase 2 was the only predictor of a positive phase 3, and 26% of the phase 3 trials were positive. [Liang et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejca.2019.08.006) **[abstract]**. Among drugs that reached phase 3, 87% of later-approved programmes had shown proof of concept in phase 2 against 28% of failed ones, and 57% against 16% used a biomarker to select patients. [Jardim et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ctrv.2016.10.009) **[abstract]**
- Programme-level models: for phase 3 to approval, an area under the curve of 0.81, with the leading features being trial outcomes, trial status, accrual rates, duration, prior approval for another indication and sponsor track record. [Lo et al. 2019](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/ct67j043) **[full]**. Trial outcome and status are known only after readout, so that figure is not a bar for a pre-readout forecaster. Trials that select patients by biomarker have higher success probabilities. [Wong et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6409418/) **[full]**. Number of endpoints and complexity of eligibility criteria are associated with phase success. [Feijoo et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drudis.2019.12.014) **[abstract]**. A four-factor model (activity, pivotal phase 2 size, phase 2 duration, prevalence) predicts oncology approval after phase 2. [DiMasi et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.194) **[abstract]**. A vendor reports 79% accuracy on prospectively published phase 2 forecasts. [Aliper et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.3008) **[abstract]**; vendor-authored, phase 2.
- Interim analyses: an announcement that a trial continues after an interim look carries quantifiable information, because it rules out both overwhelming benefit and futility. A worked case put the probability of eventual success at 48% after the third interim analysis. [Broglio et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1186/1745-6215-15-73) **[abstract]**

**Strength.** Good that structured fields alone discriminate weakly, that sponsor type and endpoint type (the project's reference class) are among the larger splits, and that a positive phase 2 is the most replicated trial-specific predictor. Weak on whether language models add anything beyond those inputs: the one platform that tests it found a random forest level with them.

## 9. Failure modes to design against

- **Leakage through retrieval.** Date-filtered search is unreliable in retrospective tests: at least one retrieved page carried major post-cutoff information for 71% of questions on Google and 81% on DuckDuckGo, and forecasts built on leaky pages scored 0.10 against 0.24 on clean ones. [El Lahib et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00758) **[abstract]**. Even with filters, audits by a second model found leaks in about 1.65% of search results in one system and 1.5% of results kept after filtering in another, where at least one flagged result appeared in 18% of questions that used web search. [Alur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07678), [Murphy](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18576) **[full]**. In the prospective arm the readout does not exist, so the exposure is narrower: an existing public result that screening missed, and partial disclosures. In a manual check of 50 trials that CT Open's automated screen had passed, one had partial results visible only in a photographed poster in a LinkedIn post.
- **Agents hunting for the answer.** In CT Open, open-weight agents "spent much of their query budget searching for unpublished outcomes" despite instructions. [Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16742) **[full]**
- **Recall mistaken for skill.** Section 8 (Windisch; CT Open's Opus 4.5 result). Stated cutoffs are guidelines; the release date is the only hard bound and a buffer of a few months is prudent. [Paleka et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00723) **[full]**
- **Copying other forecasts.** Human or market forecasts reachable through retrieval make a claim of skill circular. [Paleka et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00723) **[full]**. The AIA team blocked prediction-market domains for this reason. For trials the equivalents are analyst probability-of-success estimates and event-market prices.
- **Overconfidence and hedging.** Both occur (section 3); stated intervals are reliably too narrow (section 7).
- **Spin.** Spin appeared in 40% of trial abstract conclusions and 47% of press releases in one cohort, and spin in the abstract was the only predictor of spin in the release. [Yavchitz et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1001308) **[abstract]**. All 22 language models tested were more swayed by spun abstracts than human readers were; they could detect spin when asked, and prompting reduced its effect. [Yun et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.07963) **[full]** for the abstract and introduction. Models also tend to agree with the views put to them. [Sharma et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548) **[abstract]**. No study measures whether sponsor press language shifts a model's trial forecast.
- **Prompt wording.** Single prompt changes moved Brier by between about -0.02 and +0.03, and the mean forecast ranged from 38% to 50% across prompts. [Schoenegger et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01578) **[full]**. Models carry a default lean (toward yes in one study, toward low probabilities in another) that interacts with the base rate of the question set. [Schoenegger et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19379), [Pratt et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04446) **[full]**
- **Run-to-run variance.** Section 2. One run per trial is not a stable forecast, and two runs can sit on opposite sides of the base rate.
- **Misusing comparable trials.** Section 1 (CT Open's failure analysis).
- **Correlated outcomes.** A forecaster that commits to one view of a shared factor can top a leaderboard by luck. [Paleka et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.00723) **[full]**. Trials of one drug, or one class in one setting, are the trial-domain case.
- **Label noise and the log score.** With imperfect resolutions, clipping probabilities scores better under the log score than reporting the true probability. Same source **[full]**
- **Results that date.** Several findings come from GPT-4-era models. The Metaculus series finds the model matters more than the scaffold, so a design tuned on one model generation may not transfer to the next.

## Recommended design choices for the first version

1. **Give the forecaster the base rate as a number and combine outside the model.** Compute the reference-class base rate from the reference set, show it to the model with the instruction to adjust for this trial, and form the sealed probability as a blend of the model's answer and the base rate with a weight fixed before sealing. *Evidence:* a base-rate lookup beat oncologists (Benjamin et al.); four of five frontier models lost to a base rate closed-book (KalshiBench); supplying or blending an outside number helped in ForecastBench, Halawi et al. and the AIA report; arithmetic blending beat asking the model to update (Schoenegger et al. 2024). *Weakness:* no study tests base-rate anchoring for language models on trials, and the prompt instruction alone is worth at most 0.01-0.02. The blend weight cannot be estimated from the trials available; it is a judgement to fix in advance and examine in the retrospective pilot. It also trades risk for reach: a heavy weight on the base rate protects against losing to it and limits how far the forecaster can beat it, which bears directly on the registered claim.

2. **Retrieve prior evidence into fixed fields, then compare against several distinct trials one difference at a time.** For each trial collect, as structured records: earlier-phase results for the same drug and setting (endpoint, hazard ratio or response rate, size, randomised or single-arm), sister trials, results for the class in the same setting, the design hazard ratio where public, and any interim-analysis announcements. Have the model state how the target differs from each comparator in population, arms and endpoint before it forecasts. Start with about five to fifteen items per trial. *Evidence:* a positive phase 2 is the most replicated predictor (Monzon, Liang, Jardim); interim announcements carry information (Broglio); raw retrieval of similar trials was inconsistent and failed on design differences (CT Open); stepwise comparison from distinct anchors beat raw retrieval (DeepImagine); returns flatten after 5-15 documents (Halawi, OpenForecaster). *Weakness:* the stepwise result is one benchmark round by the benchmark's authors, scored by macro-F1 on a different question type.

3. **Average five to ten independent runs by a plain mean, and vary the evidence between runs, not only the seed.** Log the spread across runs for each trial. Do not use a language model to choose or merge runs. *Evidence:* AIA report, BLF, Halawi et al., Metaculus practice; different anchors beat repeated runs (DeepImagine); diversity is what makes deliberation pay (Schneider and Schramm, InfoDelphi). *Weakness:* the exact number is a cost judgement between five and ten. Nothing is published on aggregating numeric distributions; averaging quantiles on the log hazard-ratio scale is a reasonable default with no evidence behind it.

4. **Apply no fitted calibration in the first version; at most one parameter, fixed before sealing.** Clip probabilities to the same range the log score is clipped to. If the retrospective pilot shows a clear and consistent distortion, correct it with a single slope on the logit distance from the base rate, chosen once and written into the forecaster version. Do not use isotonic regression or binning, and do not import an extremising coefficient from general-event benchmarks. *Evidence:* a fixed parameter beat fitted ones out of sample and isotonic regression did worse (AIA report); no gain on the strongest model or when an informative prior was supplied (BLF); flexible calibration overfits below roughly 200-1,000 cases (Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana; Van Calster et al.); the direction of error differs between studies. *Weakness:* centring a correction on the base rate instead of 0.5 is this note's inference, untested.

5. **Build the hazard-ratio forecast from the reference set's empirical distribution and widen the model's interval by a measured factor.** Start from the distribution of reported log hazard ratios in the trial's reference class. Let the model move the centre using the trial's evidence, with earlier-phase effects shrunk first. Elicit several quantiles, not a point with a margin. Then set the 80% interval width from held-out errors (reference-set or pilot residuals), never from the model's stated width. *Evidence:* stated intervals cover far less than claimed and conformal widening repairs it (FermiEval, Hobor et al.); a model prior is worth about five samples and helps most when combined with data (OpenEstimate); experts' intervals on trial effects were too narrow (Atanasov et al.); design assumptions run optimistic by about 8% in the hazard ratio for industry trials, with a wide spread (Noticewala et al.; Gan et al.); phase 2 effects shrink by about a quarter (Liang et al.). *Weakness:* nothing has been published on language-model hazard-ratio forecasts. Width recalibration needs a calibration sample exchangeable with the target trials, and published trials are a selected group. The 1.46 yardstick in section 7 is arithmetic done here.

6. **Keep one plain, frozen prompt, and spend effort on evidence and model choice.** Ask for reasons for and against and a final probability. Do not instruct the model to perform Bayesian updates with numbers, to generate several candidate forecasts and select one, or to reason at maximum effort by default. Change the prompt only against a measured difference in the pilot. *Evidence:* Schoenegger et al. 2025; Pratt et al.; explicit Bayes in BLF; extended reasoning in KalshiBench; Metaculus on model against scaffold. *Weakness:* the prompt studies were closed-book on general questions; none tested trial forecasting.

7. **Treat the retrieval log as the leak, spin and independence control.** Screen every trial for a public readout before any web run. Log each page with its fetch date and stated date, and after the readout run a second-model audit of the log for pre-existing results. Tell the agent not to search for the result. Tag third-party forecasts (analyst probabilities, event-market prices) as a separate evidence class so their contribution can be reported. Pass sponsor material through an extraction step that keeps design facts and numbers and drops characterisation. *Evidence:* leak rates and audits (El Lahib et al., AIA report, BLF); agents hunting for outcomes (CT Open); piggybacking (Paleka et al.); models are more swayed by spin than people and can be prompted against it (Yun et al.); spin is common in releases (Yavchitz et al.). *Weakness:* the spin-stripping step is untested in forecasting, and no study shows sponsor language shifting a trial forecast.

8. **Do not fine-tune a language model; if anything is fitted to the reference set, make it the registry-feature model and feed its output in as evidence.** *Evidence:* no fine-tuning result below about 6,000 examples (section 6); classical models work at 100-200 labelled trials (AutoCT) and matched language models on CT Open; registry features alone explain little (Yamamoto et al.), so expect a small contribution. *Weakness:* whether worked examples from the reference set placed in the prompt help is untested.

## Not verified

- Mellers et al. 2014 was not opened; the claim rests on a search snippet.
- Atanasov and colleagues' "Human Forest vs. Random Forest" comparison for COVID-19 trial forecasts could not be opened (the SSRN page was blocked). Only the grant description was found, so no result from it is used here.
- Halawi et al. print the root-mean-square calibration error as ".42" for the system and ".38" for the crowd in both the HTML and the PDF. The units are unclear, so the figure is not quoted above.
- The score of ForecastBench's own multi-model ensemble baseline was not located in the tables read.
- The AIA report's run-count curve was read as text describing a figure, and its market-ensemble regression numbers were not extracted. It is a company technical report. KalshiBench and BLF are single-author preprints. DeepImagine shares authors with CT Open.
- Baron et al.'s table values were reconstructed from extracted text that had dropped decimal points (for example "2043" read as 2.43).
- Karvetski et al. was read in a pre-publication manuscript with a different title; only its abstract and introduction were read.
- Windisch et al. was read through the fetch tool's summary of the full-text page.
- Abstract-only sources are marked as such; their numbers come from the abstract and were not checked against tables.
- CT Open's Summer 2026 prospective leaderboard was not checked in this task.
